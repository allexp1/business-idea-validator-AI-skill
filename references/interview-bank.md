# Customer Interview Bank

Used by the 7-Day Validation Plan (nascent). Give the founder **actual
questions**, never "go talk to customers." Include the 5–8 most relevant in the
brief.

Two governing rules.

**Ask about the past, not the future.** What someone did last month is evidence.
What they say they would do is a wish.

**Make them choose, not estimate.** Every question about money should present
options and take an answer, rather than inviting a number out of the air.
Choice-based elicitation reduces hypothetical bias; open-ended valuation
maximizes it.

---

## Pain — is the problem real?

1. "Tell me about the last time you [did the job]. Walk me through what happened."
2. "What was the hardest part of that?"
3. "What have you already tried? What worked, what didn't?"
4. "How often does this come up — daily, weekly, quarterly?"
5. "What did it cost you the last time it went wrong?" *(time, money, or a relationship — any answer is useful; no answer means no pain)*

## Willingness to pay — vitamin or painkiller?

6. "What do you spend on tools or services in this area today?"
7. "Who signs off on a $X/month purchase like this?"
8. **Choice-based, not open-ended.** Do not ask "what would you pay?" Read a
   ladder instead: "At $80 a month, would you buy? At $120? At $160?" and stop
   where they stop. This is the Gabor-Granger form, and it matters — the
   meta-analysis below found choice-based elicitation measurably reduces bias,
   while an open question invites a number the person has not had to commit to.
9. "Would you pay for it today, if I could deliver it today?" *(the strongest verbal signal there is)*
10. "What budget line would this come out of?" *(no line = no purchase, regardless of enthusiasm)*

## Alternatives — the real competition

11. "What else have you evaluated or used?"
12. "If you did nothing and carried on as you are, what happens?" *(non-consumption is the most common competitor and the most commonly ignored)*
13. "What would have to be true for you to switch from what you use now?"
14. "Who else in the organization would have to agree?"

## AI-specific — new, and now essential

15. "Have you already tried doing this with ChatGPT or Claude? What happened?"
    *If they got 80% of the way there for free, that is the product's real
    competition — and its real price ceiling.*
16. "How wrong can this be before it's useless to you?" *(sets the accuracy bar
    for check 7 — most founders have never asked)*
17. "If it made a mistake, who carries that?" *(liability is often the actual
    product being purchased)*
18. "Would you let this act on its own, or does someone need to approve each
    time?" *(determines whether it is an agent or an assistant, which determines
    the pricing model)*

## By capital path

**Bootstrap:** add — "Where do you and people like you hang out online?"
Distribution is the binding constraint; every interview must also be channel
research.

**Venture:** add — "How many other teams in your company have this same problem?"
Expansion revenue is the whole model; NRR gets scored, not just logos.

**Service-as-software:** interview the *owner*, not the user — "What would you
pay to have this outcome delivered, rather than a tool to do it yourself?"

## Red flags during interviews

- **"I'd definitely pay for that."** Discount it. **How much:** the best available
  anchor is Murphy, Allen, Stevens and Weatherhead's meta-analysis of 83
  observations across 28 stated-preference studies, which found a median
  hypothetical-to-actual ratio of **1.35** — so stated willingness to pay runs
  roughly a third above what people actually do. Two caveats to state whenever
  you use it: that literature is mostly environmental valuation rather than
  software pricing, so it anchors rather than settles; and the distribution is
  severely right-skewed, meaning some respondents overstate by far more.
  Earlier versions of this file asserted a ~40% discount with no source. 1.35 is
  the sourced figure, and it is *less* pessimistic. See `academic-sources.md`.
- **Vague on "the last time it happened."** The pain is not acute. This is the single most reliable negative signal.
- **"This is exactly what I've been looking for" in the first two minutes.** They are being polite, or they are pitching you.
- **Multiple approvers needed.** Committee complexity, long cycles — fine for venture, usually fatal for bootstrap.
- **They ask for a discount before pricing has been discussed.** Interest in the price, not the problem.
- **They suggest features.** Engagement, but not evidence. Steer back to what they did last month.

## Green flags

- They have already built a spreadsheet, a script, or a manual workaround.
- They can name the amount it costs them, unprompted.
- They ask when they can have it, then ask who else is using it.
- They introduce you to a colleague without being asked.
- They describe the problem with visible irritation. Emotional intensity is the most transferable signal there is.

## Running the interviews

- **20 is the number.** Under 10 is anecdote; the patterns appear between 12 and 20.
- **Not friends or family.** They will lie to protect the relationship, kindly.
- **Do not pitch.** The moment you describe the solution, the interview is over and you are hearing politeness.
- **Record exact words.** The phrases they use are the landing-page copy, and they are better than anything that can be written for them.
- **Count budget holders, not conversations.** Ten users and zero buyers is a zero.
