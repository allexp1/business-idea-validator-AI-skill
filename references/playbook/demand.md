# Demand — proving someone will pay

Reach for this when **demand evidence** scored low, which is the most common
weak component and the one where founders most reliably fool themselves.

The whole discipline compresses to one sentence: **evidence is what could have
come out otherwise.** A conversation that could only have ended in encouragement
produced no evidence, however many of them you had.

---

## 1. Establish where you actually are

Score the strongest evidence that exists today, not the sum of all activity:

| You have | That is worth |
| --- | --- |
| Paying customers retained past three months | Real demand. Move to `pricing.md`. |
| Signed LOIs, pre-sales, a paid pilot | Strong. Money changed hands or was contractually promised. |
| 20+ interviews with consistent unprompted pain and named budget holders | Good problem evidence, zero purchase evidence. |
| Search volume, active communities complaining, people hacking workarounds | Indirect. Useful, not sufficient. |
| A waitlist, survey results, "everyone loves it" | Approximately nothing. |
| Founder conviction | Nothing. |

A waitlist measures curiosity at a price of zero. It is the single most
over-weighted signal in early-stage startups.

## 2. Measure fit if a product already exists — the Ellis test

Ask users who have experienced the core value: **"How would you feel if you
could no longer use this?"** Very disappointed / Somewhat / Not disappointed.

**≥40% "very disappointed"** is the working threshold for product-market fit.

*Provenance, stated honestly:* this is Sean Ellis's heuristic, benchmarked
across roughly a hundred startups and published on his blog in 2009. It is a
practitioner rule of thumb with real predictive history behind it, **not a
peer-reviewed finding.** Cite it as such.

**The part most people skip.** The score is a thermometer; the segmentation is
the medicine. Rahul Vohra's Superhuman work is the canonical worked example of
moving 22% → 58%:

1. Segment respondents. Ignore the "not disappointed" group entirely — they will
   never love it and building for them dilutes the product.
2. Read *what the "very disappointed" group has in common.* That description,
   not your original ICP, is your market.
3. Ask the "somewhat disappointed" group the one question that pays: **"What is
   the main benefit you receive, and what would have to improve for you to move
   to very disappointed?"**
4. Build half toward what the somewhat group asked for, half deepening what the
   very group already loves. Re-measure on a cadence.

This is also a practitioner source. Its value is the procedure, not the numbers.

## 3. If no product exists — buy a real no

Every method below is ranked by how hard it is for the participant to be polite.

1. **Pre-sale.** Take money now for something that does not exist, with a refund
   promise. Nothing else is close.
2. **Paid pilot.** A small invoice for a manual version. Also tests whether the
   outcome is worth paying for independently of your software.
3. **Letter of intent** with a named price and a named signatory.
4. **Concierge / Wizard of Oz.** Deliver the outcome by hand for five customers.
   You learn the real workflow and whether the outcome is even valued.
5. **Landing page with a checkout that fails gracefully** at the payment step.
   Measures intent-to-purchase rather than intent-to-hear-more.
6. **Ad test.** Cheapest, weakest. Measures curiosity about a claim.

Anything above line 4 is evidence. Below it is a signal.

## 4. Discount what people say

Stated willingness to pay overstates revealed willingness. The best available
anchor is a **median hypothetical-to-actual ratio of about 1.35** (Murphy,
Allen, Stevens & Weatherhead, 2005 — 83 observations across 28 studies), so
treat stated figures as running roughly a third high.

Two caveats to carry with the number every time: that literature is mostly
environmental valuation rather than software, so it anchors rather than settles;
and the distribution is severely right-skewed, so individual respondents can
overstate by far more than a third.

The same paper found **choice-based elicitation reduces the bias**, which is why
`interview-bank.md` now uses a price ladder instead of asking people to name a
figure.

## 5. Turn it into hypotheses

The discipline with actual randomized-trial support (Camuffo et al., see
`frameworks.md §12`) is: state the theory, derive falsifiable hypotheses, test
in cheapness order, and **terminate when the evidence says so.**

Write each as: *"We believe [segment] will [observable action] because [reason].
We are wrong if fewer than [N] of [M] do it by [date]."*

That sentence is also the kill criterion. They are the same object, which is why
the brief can generate both from one piece of work.

## What "fixed" looks like

Demand evidence moves out of the weak band when someone who could have said no
said yes, at a price, more than once, in a segment you can describe without
using the word "everyone".
