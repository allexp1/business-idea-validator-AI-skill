# Selling — the face-to-face institutional sale

Reach for this when the buyer is an organisation rather than a credit card:
venues, museums, clinics, councils, schools, mid-market companies. The motion is
a different craft from a growth channel, and treating it as one is why technical
founders stall at the first meeting.

Two of the three sources here are genuinely empirical, which is unusual for sales
literature and worth leaning on.

---

## 1. In a large sale, questions beat pitching

**Neil Rackham, *SPIN Selling*.** Empirical: 35,000 sales calls, 10,000
salespeople, 23 countries, 12 years, 40 researchers. The largest study of sales
effectiveness conducted, and its central finding contradicts most sales training.

In small sales, closing technique helps. **In large sales it does not.** What
predicts outcome is the sequence of questions:

- **Situation** — facts about their current setup. Necessary, and average
  performers ask far too many. Do the homework instead; every situation question
  you ask is one the buyer knows you could have looked up.
- **Problem** — difficulties and dissatisfactions. This is where most founders
  stop, then pitch.
- **Implication** — *what does that problem cost you?* Knock-on effects, in the
  buyer's own words. **Top performers ask markedly more of these**, and it is the
  question type average performers most consistently skip.
- **Need-payoff** — *what would it be worth if that went away?* This gets the
  buyer to articulate the value, which is far more durable than you asserting it.

**The one thing to take from this file:** the buyer must say the cost out loud.
A problem the buyer has named but never priced does not get budget. Implication
questions are how the price gets named, and they are the difference between a
pleasant meeting and a purchase order.

## 2. Rapport is not the winning profile

**Dixon & Adamson, *The Challenger Sale*.** Empirical: 6,000 reps across 90
companies. Five profiles — Hard Worker, Challenger, Relationship Builder, Lone
Wolf, Reactive Problem Solver.

The finding that inverts folk wisdom: roughly **40% of high performers are
Challengers, and only about 7% are Relationship Builders**, in complex sales.
Being liked is not the mechanism.

The Challenger motion is **teach, tailor, take control**:

- **Teach** the buyer something about their own business they did not know.
  Requires you to know the category better than they do — which a founder
  usually can, and usually fails to use.
- **Tailor** the insight to the person in front of you. The director of visitor
  experience and the finance lead need different versions of the same truth.
- **Take control** of the conversation, including price and next steps. Not
  aggression: refusing to leave a meeting without a defined next action.

Also from that research: around **53% of B2B loyalty is driven by the sales
experience itself** — more than product, brand or price combined. For a founder
who believes a better product will eventually win, this is the number to sit
with.

## 3. Institutional buying has three people, not one

In a venue, a museum, a clinic or a council, the roles separate:

- **The champion** — feels the pain, has no budget. Usually who you meet first.
- **The budget holder** — has money, does not feel the pain, and has never heard of you.
- **The blocker** — IT, legal, procurement, or a curator with an opinion. Can
  stop the deal and cannot start it.

**Most first meetings are with the champion, and most founders mistake champion
enthusiasm for a sale.** That is Eisenmann's False Positive arriving in a
meeting room.

Practical rules:
- Ask in the first meeting: *who else has to agree, and what would they need to see?*
- Arm the champion with the internal case, in a form they can forward without you.
- Meet the blocker early and deliberately. A blocker discovered at signature costs a quarter.
- Never let a meeting end without a named next action, an owner and a date.

## 4. Objections, and what they actually mean

Most objections are not the objection.

| They say | Usually means | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Too expensive" | Value not yet articulated **by them** | Back to implication questions. Price is only high relative to an unstated cost. |
| "Send me some information" | Polite exit | "Happy to — what specifically would make it worth a second conversation?" |
| "We're happy with what we have" | Status quo is the real competitor | Ask what it would take to make the current setup unacceptable. |
| "Not this budget cycle" | Real, or a soft no | Ask when the cycle opens and what has to be in the request. Vague answers mean soft no. |
| "Let me discuss internally" | Unnamed stakeholder | Find out who, and offer to join. |

## 5. Pilots that produce a decision

A pilot that can be extended will be extended, forever. Design it to end in a
yes or a no:

- **Charge for it.** Free pilots have no internal owner and no urgency.
- **Fix the end date** before it starts, in writing.
- **Agree the success metric before day one**, with the budget holder present.
  Not "see how it goes."
- **Agree what happens on success** — a signed order, at a stated price.
- **Keep it small enough to finish.** Most failed pilots failed on scope.

An unpaid, open-ended pilot with no agreed metric is not a sales stage. It is a
free consulting engagement with a hopeful ending.

## What "fixed" looks like

A first meeting where the buyer articulates the cost of the problem in their own
numbers, you leave with a named next action and a date, and you know who else
must agree and what they need to see.
