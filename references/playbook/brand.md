# Brand — being findable and being thought of

Reach for this when the product is genuinely fine and nobody can find it, when
check 10 fails on answer-engine presence, or when a founder is about to spend
scarce money on "awareness".

This is the one part of marketing with a real empirical literature, and it
contradicts startup folklore in several places.

**Read the transfer caveat first (§4).** The evidence base is largely
advertisers with budgets. The *mechanisms* transfer to a pre-revenue company.
The *spend ratios* do not.

---

## 1. Growth comes from penetration, not loyalty

**Byron Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, *How Brands Grow*.** Empirical
laws derived across categories and decades.

- **Brands grow by acquiring more buyers**, not by deepening existing ones.
  Loyalty follows size; it does not cause it.
- **Double jeopardy:** smaller brands have fewer buyers *and* those buyers are
  slightly less loyal. Both together, as a function of size. A small brand
  running a loyalty programme to escape being small is fighting an empirical
  regularity rather than a fixable defect.
- **Light buyers matter more than heavy ones in aggregate**, and they are the
  hardest to reach because they have heard of you least.

**The startup translation:** "we'll focus on our power users and grow through
retention" is the most comfortable strategy available and the evidence is against
it as a *growth* mechanism. Retention keeps what you have. Penetration is what
makes you bigger.

## 2. Mental and physical availability

Sharp's central claim: purchase is mostly decided by whether a brand is **thought
of** in the buying moment and **easy to buy** when it is — ahead of persuasive
differentiation.

- **Mental availability** — the probability of being noticed or recalled in a
  buying situation. Built by reaching all category buyers consistently, with
  **distinctive assets** (a name, a look, a phrase) that are recognisably yours.
- **Physical availability** — being easy to find and buy at the moment of intent.

**This is the best available explanation for answer-engine absence.** When a
buyer asks an assistant to recommend options, what surfaces is what is mentally
available *to the model* — synthesised from where the category is discussed.
Absence is not a ranking problem to be optimised; it is an availability problem,
and the fix is being present and distinctly described where the synthesis
happens. See `distribution.md §5`.

**Distinctiveness beats differentiation.** Being recognisably yours matters more
than being meaningfully different — which sits in productive tension with
`positioning.md`, and both are true: positioning decides what you say,
distinctiveness decides whether anyone knows it was you who said it.

## 3. The brand / activation split, and the right number for B2B

**Les Binet and Peter Field**, IPA Databank: 996 campaigns, 700 brands, 83
sectors, 30 years of effectiveness data.

- Long-term brand building and short-term activation do different jobs. Activation
  converts existing demand now; brand building creates demand later. Activation
  alone shows decaying returns.
- The famous ratio is **60% brand / 40% activation** — **and that is the B2C
  number.**
- **For B2B, their own later cut of the data puts efficiency nearer 46% brand /
  54% activation.** Use this one for a B2B subject. Quoting the B2C 60/40 at a
  B2B company is precisely the kind of borrowed-number error this skill exists to
  catch.
- Other B2B findings from that work: **share of voice above share of market
  predicts growth**, and **acquisition beats loyalty** — consistent with Sharp.
- Emotional campaigns outperform rational ones over the long run.

## 4. The transfer caveat — read before spending anything

This literature studies organisations with media budgets. A pre-revenue company
has no budget to split 46/54, and applying the ratio at that stage would be
following advice derived from a situation you are not in.

**What transfers:** penetration over loyalty. Mental availability as the
mechanism. Distinctive assets, which cost consistency rather than money. Share of
voice as a relative measure — in a small niche, being the most visible voice can
cost very little. Acquisition over retention as the early priority.

**What does not transfer:** the spend ratio, brand campaigns with no
attributable response, and anything justified as "building awareness" without a
named audience and a way to know it worked.

**The honest version for a founder with limited runway:** you cannot buy mental
availability. You can earn a narrow slice of it by being consistently present and
distinctly described in the two or three places your category is actually
discussed. That is the applicable part.

## 5. Why things get talked about

**Jonah Berger, *Contagious*** (Wharton). Word of mouth is not luck; it has
identifiable drivers — **STEPPS**: social currency (sharing makes the sharer look
good), triggers (cues in the environment that bring you to mind), emotion (high-
arousal emotion travels; sadness does not), public visibility, practical value,
and stories.

**Triggers** are the underused one and overlap directly with mental availability:
tie yourself to something that recurs in the buyer's environment, and you are
recalled without spending anything.

**Social currency** is the test for anything you publish: does sharing this make
the sharer look informed to their peers? If not, it will not travel however good
it is.

## What "fixed" looks like

A distinctive asset used consistently enough to be recognised; presence in the
two or three places your category is discussed and synthesised; and an
acquisition-first plan that is not quietly a retention plan.
