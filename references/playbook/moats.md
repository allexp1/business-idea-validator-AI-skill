# Moats — building one, not just measuring one

Reach for this when the **moat** component scored low, or when AI-era checks 1,
5 or 6 came back FAIL.

`frameworks.md §4` uses Porter to ask *what protects this industry's profits?*
That is diagnostic. This file asks the constructive question: **which advantage
could this specific company plausibly build, from where it stands today?**

The organizing framework is Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers* (2016). It is a
practitioner-investor work rather than peer-reviewed research, and it earns its
place because it is the only widely used framework that treats moats as things
you *construct* on a schedule.

---

## Every power needs both halves

A power is a **benefit** plus a **barrier**.

- **Benefit** — it materially improves cash flow: higher price, lower cost, or
  less capital required.
- **Barrier** — a reason a competitor who can see exactly what you are doing
  still cannot arbitrage it away.

Founders describe benefits and call them moats. "Customers love our UX" is a
benefit with no barrier. Ask of any claimed advantage: *what specifically stops
a well-funded competitor copying this next quarter?* If the answer is effort or
taste, it is not yet a power.

## The seven

| Power | Benefit | The barrier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scale economies** | Unit cost falls with volume | A challenger must match volume to match cost |
| **Network economies** | Value rises with each user | A challenger starts with an empty network |
| **Counter-positioning** | A model incumbents cannot copy | Copying would damage their existing business |
| **Switching costs** | Customers stay because leaving hurts | The cost is borne by the customer, not you |
| **Branding** | Willingness to pay above function | Built over years; cannot be bought quickly |
| **Cornered resource** | Preferential access to something valuable | Others cannot get it at the price you can |
| **Process power** | Better quality or cost from how you operate | Tacit, embedded, slow to replicate even when described |

## Which are reachable *now* — the stage question

This is the actionable part, and Helmer's power progression is the reason to use
this framework rather than a moat checklist.

**At origination — an idea or early product — only two are realistically available:**

- **Counter-positioning.** Adopt a model the incumbent will not follow because
  following damages what already pays their bills. The barrier is their rational
  self-interest, which is remarkably durable. This is the strongest power a
  small company can hold, and the most underused. Ask: what could we do that the
  obvious incumbent would be *punished* for copying?
- **Cornered resource.** Preferential access to something others cannot get on
  your terms: an exclusive data agreement, a licence, a distribution
  relationship, a person. Founders often have one and fail to name it.

**At take-off — you have real volume — three more become available:**
scale economies, network economies, switching costs. They compound with usage,
which is precisely why they cannot be built before there is usage. Moat work
before traction is theatre.

**Branding and process power are late.** Both take years. Claiming either at
seed stage is a tell.

**So the honest answer to "how do we build a moat" is stage-dependent**, and a
playbook that lists all seven to a pre-launch founder has wasted their time. Name
the two that are reachable, and say plainly that the others come later.

## In the AI era specifically

Check 1 asks whether you own a complement the model labs need to keep scarce.
The labs want everything adjacent to the model cheap and abundant, so the
question is which power survives that pressure:

- **Cornered resource** and **counter-positioning** survive best early —
  regulated access, exclusive data, a channel relationship, a model the labs'
  own business would be damaged by adopting.
- **Switching costs** are where workflow ownership (check 6) pays off. Being the
  system of record *is* a switching cost. This is the most commonly available
  route from a weak moat score to a real one, and usually the right
  recommendation for a vertical product.
- **Scale economies** are weak here, because inference costs fall for everyone.
- **Network economies** rarely apply to single-player tools, and founders
  claiming them usually mean "more data", which is check 5, not a network.

Pair with `archetypes-2026.md`: the compounding archetype is vertical workflow
ownership plus regulated access, which is switching costs plus cornered
resource, arrived at from the other direction.

## What "fixed" looks like

One named power, with its barrier stated in a sentence that survives the
question *why can't a funded competitor do this next quarter?*, and which is
actually reachable from the company's current stage.
