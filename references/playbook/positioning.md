# Positioning — what you say, and who to

Reach for this when the **no-response diagnostic** in `index.md` lands on
*wrong message*, or when a channel with real volume produced silence.

The distinction this file exists to serve: **"nobody wants this" and "nobody
understood what you said" produce identical symptoms and opposite
interventions.** One means stop. The other means change six words and try again.
Do not conclude the first until you have tested the second, because the second
is cheaper by an order of magnitude.

---

## 1. Positioning has parts, and they are diagnosable

April Dunford's framework (*Obviously Awesome*, 2019 — practitioner, not
research, and the most useful practical treatment available). Positioning is not
a slogan; it is five decisions that constrain each other:

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what the customer would genuinely do if you did
   not exist. Not your competitor list: their actual fallback, which is usually a
   spreadsheet, an intern, or nothing.
2. **Unique attributes** — what you have that the alternatives do not. Features,
   not adjectives.
3. **Value** — what those attributes let the customer *do* that they could not before.
4. **Who cares most** — the customers for whom that value is urgent rather than pleasant.
5. **Market category** — the frame you ask to be judged in, which sets every
   expectation the buyer arrives with.

**The diagnostic use:** when a message fails, one of these five is usually wrong,
and they fail in a recognisable order. Start at (1). Founders almost always list
competitors rather than alternatives, and everything downstream inherits the
error — you end up differentiating against companies the buyer has never heard
of, while the real rival is the status quo.

Note that (1) is the **Added Value** question from Phase 2 arriving from the
marketing side. If the honest answer there was "they use something slightly
worse", positioning cannot rescue it. That is a demand finding, not a message one.

## 2. Match the message to what the buyer already knows

Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966 — practitioner, and still the
sharpest thing written on this).

**Five stages of awareness.** The buyer is at exactly one of these, and the
message must meet them there:

| Stage | They know | The message must |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Nothing; no felt problem | Name the problem they have not articulated |
| Problem-aware | They hurt; no idea solutions exist | Validate the pain, show a fix exists |
| Solution-aware | Solutions exist; haven't chosen | Compare approaches, introduce yours |
| Product-aware | They know you; not convinced | Prove it, with evidence |
| Most-aware | Convinced; need a reason now | Make the offer, remove friction |

**The common failure is a product-aware message sent to a problem-aware buyer.**
It reads as noise because it answers a question they have not yet asked. Most
cold outbound fails this way: it opens with what the product does, to someone who
has not conceded they have a problem.

## 3. Market sophistication — the model that explains crowded categories

Schwartz's second and less-known model: **how many times has this market already
heard your claim?**

| Level | The market | What still works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | First to make any claim | State the claim plainly. It lands. |
| 2 | Claim is familiar | Enlarge it — bigger, faster, cheaper |
| 3 | Enlargement exhausted | Lead with the **mechanism**: *why* yours works |
| 4 | Mechanism claims exhausted | Elaborate the mechanism, or make it new |
| 5 | Everything exhausted; cynical | Sell **identity** — who this is for, and who they become |

**This is the framework for anyone in a crowded AI category**, and it converts
"we can't get replies" from a mystery into a prediction. When a dozen companies
all say "AI-powered, 30 languages, set up in minutes", the market is at level 4
or 5. At that level a plain claim is *structurally* invisible — not weak, not
badly worded, invisible — and no amount of subject-line testing fixes a
level-1 message aimed at a level-4 market.

The two exits are the same two the table gives: a **mechanism** nobody else can
claim, or an **identity** so specific that the buyer recognises themselves.

**It also explains answer-engine absence.** Systems that synthesise
recommendations surface what is distinctly described. A claim identical to eleven
competitors has nothing to surface on.

## 4. The category decision

Three moves, and the right one depends on sophistication and on your resources:

- **Fight for share** in the existing category. Only viable with a real mechanism
  advantage or more money than the incumbents. Rarely right for a small company.
- **Narrow to a beachhead** — win one segment completely rather than a thin
  slice of everyone. Nearly always right for bootstrap. A smaller frame makes
  your attributes look decisive instead of marginal.
- **Reframe the category** — ask to be judged by different criteria. Powerful
  and expensive: you take on the cost of teaching the market a new frame, and
  most companies underestimate it by years.

## 5. Testing a message properly

Change **one thing at a time**, or you learn nothing:

1. Same list, new first line and subject only. If response moves, it was message.
2. Same message, hand-picked perfect-fit list of 20, sent personally. If that
   moves, it was targeting.
3. Same list, same message, different **offer shape** — done-for-you, paid pilot,
   audit. If that moves, it was the offer, not the words.

If all three produce silence, stop testing messages. That is a demand finding
and it belongs back in `demand.md`, or in the terminate column of the diagnostic.

## What "fixed" looks like

A message a stranger in the target segment can repeat back after one read; a
stated competitive alternative that is the buyer's real fallback; and a claim
that is not interchangeable with the eleven other companies in the category.
