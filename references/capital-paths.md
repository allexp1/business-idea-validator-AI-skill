# Capital Paths — which yardstick applies

**The most common way a validator gets a verdict wrong is measuring an idea
against the wrong definition of success.** A $50M-TAM niche tool judged on
venture criteria scores "Weak" while being an excellent business. A
consumer-scale venture play judged on bootstrap criteria gets waved through
while being unfundable.

Pick the path **before** scoring. State it in the brief. If the founder's stated
path and their idea's shape disagree, say so — that mismatch is often the single
most useful finding in the whole evaluation.

---

## Detecting the path

Ask directly if it is not obvious. One question is enough:

> "Is the goal a profitable business you own, a venture-scale company you raise
> for, or an operating business you buy into and improve?"

Signals when they don't say:

| Signal | Path |
| --- | --- |
| Solo or two founders, no raise mentioned, day job, "side project" | **Bootstrap** |
| "$X ARR by year 3", cap table, co-founder search, accelerator | **Venture** |
| Existing agency/practice, acquisition talk, EBITDA language, fragmented industry | **Service-as-software** |
| Niche the founder personally lives in, small named audience | **Bootstrap** |
| Requires network effects, land-grab timing, or heavy upfront capital | **Venture** (or it doesn't work) |

**Default when genuinely ambiguous: Bootstrap.** It is the cheaper mistake. A
bootstrap-scored idea that turns out venture-scale gets a happy upgrade; a
venture-scored idea that was always a lifestyle business gets a founder talked
out of something that would have worked.

## The three yardsticks

All figures carry verified dates in `market-data.md`. Re-verify before quoting.

| | **Bootstrap / indie** | **Venture** | **Service-as-software / rollup** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question being answered | "Can I own a profitable business?" | "Can this be a $100M+ company?" | "Can AI re-margin an existing book of work?" |
| Reachable market floor | ~$20M reachable niche | $1B+ TAM | Fragmented market, thousands of small operators |
| Success at 24 months | $10k MRR, profitable, sustainable solo | ~$3.5M ARR, 120%+ NRR | Acquired EBITDA plus a demonstrated margin lift |
| Gross margin target | 60%+ **after inference** | 60%+ expected (~25% is the scaling reality) | 30–40% "software-like" on a services base |
| CAC payback | **<6 months** — there is no runway to amortize | <20 months | Deal-multiple math, not CAC |
| LTV:CAC | ≥3:1, and CAC must be mostly time not cash | ≥3:1 | N/A — measured on acquisition multiple |
| Primary channel | Founder-led, community, answer-engine presence | PLG or sales-assist, paid acquisition | M&A pipeline and operator relationships |
| Team | 1–3, no hiring plan required | Hiring plan is part of the thesis | Operators + integrators, not just engineers |
| Fatal failure | Never clears $1k MRR (~70% don't) | Cannot raise the next round | Integration failure; the acquired book churns |
| Exit | Optional. Cashflow is the point | Required. Acquisition or IPO | 12–18× on re-margined EBITDA |

## What changes in the evaluation

### Bootstrap / indie

**The evidence base.** Amar Bhidé's study of Inc. 500 companies (*The Origin and
Evolution of New Businesses*, Oxford University Press, 2000) found that only
about 5% had venture capital, the typical firm started with under $30,000, 41%
began with no business plan at all, and just 6% claimed a unique product or
service. The bootstrap path is not the consolation prize; on the numbers it is
the normal way substantial companies begin. Score it as its own thing, not as a
venture plan with less money.

- **TAM stops mattering; reachability starts.** Do not compute TAM/SAM/SOM.
  Compute: how many buyers exist, can one person reach them, at what price.
- **Distribution is the whole game** and it must be a channel one person can run
  without a budget. If the plan needs paid acquisition, it is not a bootstrap plan.
- **Score the 10× test against effort, not the market.** "Is this 10× better for
  a specific small group" beats "is this 10× better than the category leader."
- **Inference cost is existential**, not a line item — there is no funded runway
  to absorb negative margin. Check 3 carries extra weight here.
- **Founder-idea fit is the largest single predictor.** Being your own customer
  substitutes for a research budget you don't have.
- **The right kill criterion is time, not money:** if it has not reached $1k MRR
  in N months of real effort, the answer is arriving.

### Venture
- TAM/SAM/SOM as written in `frameworks.md §2`.
- Score against the current Series A bar, not the bar the founder remembers.
- **Add a financing-risk paragraph.** Capital is abundant in aggregate and
  extremely concentrated in practice. If the plan needs a Series B in 2027–28,
  price the correction risk explicitly rather than assuming the window stays open.
- Retention and NRR carry more weight than growth rate; growth without NRR is a
  leaky bucket with a bigger tap.

### Service-as-software / rollup
- **Evaluate the acquisition, not the product.** Deal flow, operator quality,
  integration capacity, and the margin delta AI actually delivers.
- Unit economics become deal economics: purchase multiple, EBITDA pre- and
  post-automation, integration cost, and time to margin lift.
- The technology risk is low and the **operational risk is high** — the reverse
  of the other two paths. Score check 7 (reliability) and team fit accordingly.
- This is the fastest-growing 2026 category, which also means the most
  competition for deals. Ask what proprietary deal flow they have; "we'll find
  targets" is not an answer.

## Path mismatch — a finding in its own right

Name it explicitly when it appears. Common mismatches:

- **Bootstrap founder, venture-shaped idea** — needs network effects, a land
  grab, or two years of unpaid build. The honest advice is usually to change the
  idea, not to find investors.
- **Venture founder, bootstrap-shaped idea** — a good $40k MRR business being
  forced onto a growth curve it cannot support. Raising here is what kills it.
- **Software founder, services-shaped opportunity** — the buyer wants the
  outcome, not the tool. The better shape is to deliver the work and automate it
  behind the scenes. This is the most valuable and least welcome pivot to
  recommend, and it is right more often in 2026 than it has ever been.
